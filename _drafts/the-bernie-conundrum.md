My friend Bernie has a rule. In any long-term relationship, both partners are convinced they are doing more than their fair share. Not sometimes. Not occasionally. Always. If you poll them separately, the two numbers add up to something well over 100%, and each person is sincere.

Bernie framed it as a relationship thing, but once I started looking for it, it was everywhere. Roommates on dishes. Siblings on elder care. Coworkers on the shared drive. Co-founders on the "real work." Each person, separately, is the one carrying the weight. Nobody is lying. Everybody is wrong at the same time.

## Why Everyone Is Both Right and Wrong

The conundrum isn't really a contradiction; it's a predictable output of how attention works. You experience every second of your own effort. The alarm, the shower, the commute, the call, the fatigue, the small concessions, the mental load of remembering whose birthday is next week. You experience almost none of your partner's effort. You see its outputs, sometimes, if they happen in front of you. Usually you see the version that was already filtered for "don't mention it."

So when it comes time to estimate contribution, you are summing two very different integrals. Yours is dense. Theirs is sparse. The result is predictable and symmetric: you both come out of the estimate thinking the same thing, and the only reason it doesn't collapse into one person being obviously right is that the bias is almost perfectly mirrored.

## The Research (Because Of Course Someone Studied This)

This is a well-established effect. [Ross and Sicoly (1979)](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1979-28199-001) ran the canonical study: they asked married couples to estimate each partner's responsibility for a long list of household tasks. The contributions summed to well over 100% in the vast majority of couples. Everyone slightly (or grossly) overclaimed.

The effect has names in the literature: the **self-serving attribution bias**, the **availability heuristic** applied to memory of effort, **egocentric bias in recall**. The mechanisms are:

- **Availability.** You remember your own contributions more vividly because you were there for all of them.
- **Attentional asymmetry.** You notice your costs; you notice others' outputs. Those are different things being compared.
- **Construal.** You judge yourself by intent and effort ("I tried"), others by outcome ("did the thing get done").
- **Self-protection.** Believing you are contributing fairly is a cheaper mental state than believing you are the free-rider.

None of these require malice. They don't even require selfishness. They just require you to be a person with one set of eyes.

## It's Not Just Relationships

Bernie's version was about couples, but the same shape shows up everywhere there's shared effort and no shared ledger:

- **Work.** Everyone on the team is carrying the team. The slackers are other people.
- **Family.** Among siblings managing an aging parent, the one who visits most often is always you. Ask the other two: same answer.
- **Open source.** Every maintainer knows they are the one holding the lights on.
- **Group projects.** "I did most of the work" is the universal post-mortem.
- **Civic life.** Your political tribe is the reasonable one trying to hold the country together.

Any shared enterprise with fuzzy accounting produces the same output: a population of people each privately convinced they are above average.

## What To Do About It

The first-order move is to notice it in yourself. If your instinct is "I am doing more than my share," the probabilistic prior is that this is partially an artifact of the asymmetry above. It does not mean you are doing an equal share, but it does mean your estimate is biased upward by a known amount.

Some operational responses:

- **Lower the confidence of your own estimate.** Not to zero; you probably _are_ also doing something. Just treat your own summary with suspicion.
- **Make the invisible visible.** A shared list, a rotating schedule, an explicit rota for the mental-load items (birthdays, appointments, running low on X). Not to litigate, but to put both people's efforts in the same frame so the attention asymmetry has less to work with.
- **Reverse-audit.** Ask yourself, "what have they done this week that I only partly noticed?" This is surprisingly productive. Most of the time the list is longer than expected.
- **Assume the 120% rule.** Bernie's rule, formalized: if both partners feel they are doing 60%, the numbers are probably closer to 50/50 than either of them thinks. If one says 70 and the other says 50, it is likely close to even.
- **Don't try to win the tally.** The whole game of "I did more this week" is a trap; the bias guarantees you can always find evidence. Redirect the conversation to "what's hard right now" instead.

## Further Reading

- [Ross and Sicoly, "Egocentric Biases in Availability and Attribution" (1979)](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1979-28199-001): the foundational study on couples overclaiming household work.
- [Self-serving bias (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-serving_bias): the broader family of effects.
- [Availability heuristic (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability_heuristic): why your own effort feels bigger.
- [Naive cynicism (Kruger and Gilovich)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naive_cynicism): the related finding that people believe _others_ are more self-interested than they actually are.
