When asked about marriage advice, my very wise colleague posited the following:

<blockquote>
You'll always feel like you're doing more than the other.
<cite>— Bernie</cite>
</blockquote>

Bernie framed it as a relationship thing, but once I started looking for it, it was everywhere: across teams, between colleagues on the same team, among friends on a trip, between parents and kids. Each person, separately, feels like the one carrying the weight; nobody is lying, and yet everybody is wrong at the same time. Because, of course, I'm the one who does the most work.

This is a predictable consequence of how attention works. We experience every second of our own effort: the alarm, the shower, the commute, the call, the small concessions, the fatigue, the mental load of remembering to renew the car insurance. And we experience almost none of our partner's effort; we see only their outputs when they happen in front of us.

So, when it comes time to estimate contribution, we integrate two different quantities: ours is dense with details; theirs is sparse. Sure enough, the inevitable conclusion is that we are doing at least 60% of the work.

## Of Course, Someone Studied This!

It turns out this is a well-established effect: [Ross and Sicoly (1979)](<https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/37_J_Personality_Social_Psychology_322_(Ross).pdf>). They ran the canonical study, asking married couples to estimate each partner's responsibility for a long list of household tasks; the contributions summed to well over 100% in the vast majority of couples, because everyone slightly (or grossly) overclaimed.

The effect has names in the literature: the **self-serving attribution bias**, the **availability heuristic** applied to memory of effort, or **egocentric bias in recall**.

I call this the **Bernie Conundrum**, and it can be understood through these underlying mechanisms:

- **Availability:** We remember our own contributions more vividly because we were there for all of them.
- **Attentional asymmetry:** We notice our costs and others' outputs.
- **Construal:** We judge ourselves by intent and effort (_I tried_), others by outcome (_did the thing get done_).
- **Self-protection:** Believing we are contributing fairly is a cheaper mental state than believing we are the free-rider.

None of these require malice, or even selfishness; they only require us to be human, with all the limitations that entails. After all, aren't we all above average?

## What To Do About It?

The first-order move is to notice it in ourselves. If our instinct is _I am doing more than my share_, this is most likely partially an artifact of the asymmetry explained above. It does not mean we are doing an equal share, but it does mean our estimate is biased upward by some amount.

When this happens, we can:

- **Lower the confidence of our estimate:** not to zero, since we probably _are_ also doing something, but enough to treat our own summary with suspicion.
- **Increase visibility:** a shared list, a shared schedule, an explicit ledger for the mental-load items (birthdays, appointments, running low on X), not to litigate but to start calibrating our estimates.
- **Reverse-audit:** ask ourselves, "what have they done this week that I only partly noticed?" It takes a bit of mental effort, and often results in a list longer than expected.
- **Don't try to win the tally:** the whole game of "I did more this week" is a trap, because the bias guarantees we can always find evidence to further our claim. Instead, redirect the conversation to _What's hard right now?_
