Somewhere in a drawer I have the SD cards from every camera I have owned, a couple of hard drives pulled out of dead laptops, and a shoebox of prints. The prints are the only ones I am sure still hold what I put on them, which felt backwards enough that I went looking for what the papers and the field data say about how long each medium lasts, why it dies, and what you can do about it. Stone and paper are in here as reference points: it helps to know what the slow end of the scale looks like before arguing about whether a hard drive lasts five years or six.

## Stone

A carving stores information by removing material, and you read it by the shadow the cut casts. The mark and the medium are the same stuff, so no layer can peel or fade; the only way to lose the message is to lose the surface. That happens through weathering, which for calcite stones (limestone, marble) means dissolving in mildly acidic rain, "sugaring" as the grains loosen, and gypsum crusts spalling off the sheltered faces, while silicate stones like granite mostly shrug it off ([USGS on acid rain and stone](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/acidrain/3.html)). The rates have been measured on gravestones, because lead lettering resists weathering while the marble around it recedes, so a depth gauge against the lead gives a direct reading: about 0.2 mm per century in rural Britain, rising to around 3 mm per century in the middle of Birmingham ([a survey of 591 marble tombstones in 33 UK cemeteries](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1352231015305926)). At those rates, shallow lettering on exposed marble goes illegible in a century or two of city air. The Rosetta Stone, granodiorite lightly incised in 196 BC, is fully legible 2,200 years later, having spent most of that time buried and then built into a wall.

Density is where stone stops being a contender. One decree of about 11,000 characters is cut into it three times, on a face of 1,123 by 757 mm and a slab 284 mm thick ([dimensions per the British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone)). Counting only the unique text, that is 11 kB, one byte per 77 mm² of face, or about 5 × 10⁻⁵ bytes per mm³ of rock. Its other failure mode is people: the Georgia Guidestones, 108 tonnes of granite erected in 1980 as a message to the far future, were dynamited in July 2022 and bulldozed the same afternoon ([GBI](https://gbi.georgia.gov/press-releases/2022-07-07/gbi-investigates-explosion-elbert-county)).

## Paper

Paper is a mat of cellulose fibres with ink absorbed into it or toner fused on top, and the two fail on separate schedules: the ink fades under light, and the substrate falls apart through acid hydrolysis of the cellulose chains ([Library of Congress paper FAQ](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/about/faqs/paper.html)). Rag paper made of cotton and linen is close to neutral and lasts; the Gutenberg Bible, printed around 1455, is still on display at the Library of Congress ([LoC](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/bibles/the-gutenberg-bible.html)). Wood pulp paper from about 1850 onward is another story. Mechanical pulping leaves lignin in the sheet, and the alum-rosin sizing that keeps ink from feathering is acidic, so the paper yellows and goes brittle within a human lifetime ([PSAP guide to paper](https://psap.library.illinois.edu/collection-id-guide/paper)); by 1988 the Library of Congress estimated six million of its volumes were too brittle to circulate ([OTA report](https://swh.princeton.edu/~ota/disk2/1988/8806/880603.PDF)). Modern acid-free paper (ANSI Z39.48, ISO 9706) is rated to last "several hundred years" ([LoC](https://ask.loc.gov/preservation/faq/337512)), while a thermal paper receipt can be blank in five ([National Archives of Australia](https://www.naa.gov.au/information-management/storing-and-preserving-information/preserving-information/managing-records-thermal-papers)). So the shoebox is fine if the prints are on decent stock and the ink is pigment: Epson rates its pigment inks at up to 200 years for colour and 400 for black and white ([Epson](https://news.epson.com/news/new-epson-ultrachrome-hdx-pigment-ink-technology-delivers-the-highest-print-permanence-ratings-in-history-for-epson-ultrachrome-inks)). Dye inkjet from 2005, less so.

At around 4,000 characters per A4 page, plain text is 4 kB per sheet, or about 0.6 bytes per mm³ for a 0.1 mm thick sheet, which is ten thousand times denser than the Rosetta Stone. Print a 2D barcode instead and [PaperBack](http://ollydbg.de/Paperbak/) claims 500 kB per sheet at 600 dpi. Microfilm, which is the archivist's actual answer for anything that must be readable in 500 years, is rated LE-500 under ISO 18901: a minimum life expectancy of 500 years at 21 °C and 50% relative humidity, and US federal records rules require exactly that film ([36 CFR 1238.14](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-36/section-1238.14)).

## Hard Drives

A hard drive stores a bit as the magnetization direction of a cluster of grains in a thin film on a platter. A write head flying about 10 nm above the surface sets the grains vertically (perpendicular recording), and a tunnel-magnetoresistance sensor reads them back ([WD on ePMR](https://blog.westerndigital.com/the-magnetic-attraction-of-epmr/)). Smaller grains mean more bits per inch, and eventually grains so small that room-temperature heat flips them (the superparamagnetic limit), which is why the newest drives heat the spot with a laser for a nanosecond before writing it (HAMR) and fill the enclosure with helium so that more, thinner platters fit ([Backblaze on HAMR](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/hamr-hard-drives/)). A shipping Seagate Exos M has ten platters, twenty heads, 1.84 Tbit/in² and 32 TB ([product manual](https://www.seagate.com/content/dam/seagate/assets/support/internal-hard-drives/enterprise-hard-drives/exos-m/_shared/files/Seagate_Exos_M_SATA_Product_Manual_32-30-28-24TB_210048100D.pdf)), and 44 TB drives started shipping in March ([Seagate](https://www.seagate.com/stories/articles/seagate-delivers-industrys-highest-capacity-hard-drives-with-next-generation-mozaic-4/)). In a 3.5" box of 147 × 101.6 × 26.1 mm, 44 TB comes to about 110 MB per mm³, two hundred million times paper.

The magnetic bits are the durable part, designed to stay put for decades. What fails is everything around them. Heads crash, bearings wear, boards die, and the nanometre or two of lubricant on the platter can migrate during long storage until the parked heads stick to it. At the sector level, drives grow latent sector errors: in NetApp's fleet 3.45% of drives developed at least one unreadable sector during their life ([Schroeder et al., FAST 2010](https://static.usenix.org/events/fast10/tech/full_papers/schroeder.pdf)), and 0.66% of nearline disks returned data that silently failed a checksum within 17 months ([Bairavasundaram et al., FAST 2008](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~bianca/papers/fast08.pdf)). The datasheet quotes one nonrecoverable read error per 10¹⁵ bits, an MTBF of 2.5 million hours and a 0.35% annual failure rate, then adds that MTBF "is a population statistic not relevant to individual units".

The field data disagrees with the datasheet by a comfortable factor. Schroeder and Gibson looked at 100,000 drives and found annual replacement rates of 2 to 4% where the MTTF implied 0.88%, with wear-out starting early instead of the bathtub curve everyone draws ([FAST 2007](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~bianca/papers/fast07.pdf)). Google's study of over 100,000 consumer drives found that after a first scan error a drive is 39 times more likely to die within 60 days, that after a first reallocated sector it is 14 times more likely, and that 56% of the drives that failed never showed a count in any of the four strong SMART signals ([Pinheiro et al., FAST 2007](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/disk_failures.pdf)). It also found that cool drives failed more often, which surprised the authors, though a 2026 reanalysis of Backblaze's data finds hot ones do, about 2% more failures per degree ([Siemroth and Park](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29078)). Backblaze's own fleet of 341,000 drives runs at a 1.39% lifetime annual failure rate ([Q1 2026 Drive Stats](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-drive-stats-for-q1-2026/)); the median drive lasted six years and nine months in their 2021 analysis ([Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/how-long-do-disk-drives-last/)), and their 2025 revisit pushed the failure peak out to ten years at only 4.25% ([Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/are-hard-drives-getting-better-lets-revisit-the-bathtub-curve/)).

Unpowered is a different question, and no manufacturer rates it. Seagate's storage envelope is −40 to 70 °C and 5 to 95% humidity, preventive maintenance "none required", and no shelf life at all; the NIST guide on media longevity gives an unpowered hard drive under two years of high confidence ([NIST IR 8387](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2022/NIST.IR.8387.pdf)). Iron Mountain reports that about one in five of the 1990s hard drives that music labels send them are unreadable, often as much because the interface and the power supply are gone as because the drive is ([Mix](https://www.mixonline.com/business/inside-iron-mountain-its-time-to-talk-about-hard-drives)). LTO tape, for comparison, is rated for 30 years of archival storage ([HPE](https://hpe.com/emea_europe/en/product-catalog/options/pip.specifications.hpe-lto-ultrium-cartridges.1013249550.html)).

## Flash

An SSD or SD card stores bits as electrons trapped in an insulated layer of a transistor, which shifts the voltage at which the transistor switches on; the controller reads a cell by comparing that threshold voltage against references. SLC uses two voltage levels for one bit, MLC four for two, TLC eight for three and QLC sixteen for four, each step halving the margin between states ([Cai et al., Proc. IEEE 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.08642)). 3D NAND stacks the cells hundreds of layers high instead of shrinking them: Micron's 232-layer TLC reaches 14.6 Gbit/mm² of die ([Micron](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2022/07/26/2486015/14450/en/Micron-Ships-World-s-First-232-Layer-NAND-Extends-Technology-Leadership.html)), and SK hynix is in production at 321 layers. The result is that a 2 TB microSD card, 11 × 15 × 1 mm, comes to about 12 GB per mm³, a hundred times denser than the hard drive and ten times denser than the largest data centre SSD, Kioxia's 245 TB LC9 ([Kioxia](https://apac.kioxia.com/en-apac/business/news/2025/20250722-1.html)).

Flash fails in two ways that matter. Every program/erase cycle damages the tunnel oxide, so the cell leaks faster and the voltage windows widen; 50 nm planar MLC took 10,000 cycles, 15 nm TLC about 1,000 (Cai). And the trapped electrons leak out on their own, faster when it is warm, so data written and left unpowered fades. JEDEC's JESD218 requires a client SSD to retain data for one year at 30 °C powered off and an enterprise SSD for three months at 40 °C, both measured at the end of the drive's rated endurance ([Alvin Cox, JEDEC](https://www.jedec.org/sites/default/files/Alvin_Cox%20%5BCompatibility%20Mode%5D_0.pdf)). The model behind it is Arrhenius, and every 5 °C roughly halves retention, which is where the 2015 "SSDs lose data in days" headlines came from: slide 27 of that deck, 55 °C storage, two weeks. That applies to a fully worn drive stored in a hot car; a fresh drive retains for a decade or so ([PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/427602/debunked-your-ssd-wont-lose-data-if-left-unplugged-after-all.html)). Retention does shrink as the drive wears ([Dell](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-ca/000198930/ssd-data-retention-considerations-when-powering-off-systems-for-a-prolonged-duration)), which is the part the headlines got right.

Reading a cell also nudges the cells next to it, and the controller is a small computer that can crash mid-write. Raspberry Pi's whitepaper attributes most SD card corruption to power loss while unwritten data is still in the kernel cache or the card's own buffer ([Raspberry Pi](https://pip.raspberrypi.com/categories/685-whitepapers-app-notes/documents/RP-003610-WP/Making-a-more-resilient-file-system.pdf)); enterprise SSDs have capacitors to finish the write, consumer drives do not.

In the field, flash looks good on replacement rates and less good on error rates. Google's six-year study found that 20 to 63% of drives had a final read error in their first four years, that raw bit errors grow far slower with wear than the exponential everyone assumed, that age matters independently of cycles, and that SLC drives were no more reliable than MLC ([Schroeder et al., FAST 2016](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/fast16/fast16-papers-schroeder.pdf)). Facebook saw 4 to 34% of SSDs per platform report an uncorrectable error ([Meza et al., SIGMETRICS 2015](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~omutlu/pub/flash-memory-failures-in-the-field-at-facebook_sigmetrics15.pdf)). NetApp's 1.4 million SSDs were replaced at 0.22% per year ([Maneas et al., FAST 2020](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast20-maneas.pdf)), Alibaba's million NVMe drives at 0.98%, with 1.4% going slow without any SMART warning ([Lu et al., ATC 2022](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc22-lu.pdf)), and Backblaze's boot SSDs at 0.9% ([Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/ssd-edition-2023-mid-year-drive-stats-review/)). The Tech Report wrote to six consumer SSDs until they died: all exceeded their rating, two passed 2 PB, and the Samsung 840 died without warning with its data unrecoverable ([The SSD Endurance Experiment](https://web.archive.org/web/2015/http://techreport.com/review/27909/the-ssd-endurance-experiment-theyre-all-dead)).

SD cards are the same NAND with a cheaper brain. Bunnie Huang showed that every card contains a microcontroller, added for 15 to 30 cents, that exists to hide defects ("all flash memory is riddled with defects, without exception") and can be reflashed to lie about capacity ([On Hacking MicroSD Cards](https://www.bunniestudios.com/blog/2013/on-hacking-microsd-cards/)). Matt Cole has been writing to 311 microSD cards for two years, 57 PiB in total, and the tally is blunt: industrial cards averaged 47,800 program/erase cycles before 0.1% of sectors failed, high-endurance cards 9,900, consumer cards 7,600, fake-capacity cards 2,200, and the fakes cost more per gigabyte than the real thing ([The Great MicroSD Card Survey](https://www.bahjeez.com/the-great-microsd-card-survey-two-years-later/)). Observed failure modes include the card returning data from the wrong address and erasing itself to zeros. None of the speed classes (V30, A2) say anything about endurance; only industrial cards publish a write budget, such as 3,840 TB for Kingston's pSLC card ([datasheet](https://www.kingston.com/datasheets/sdcit2_us.pdf)).

## Side by Side

| Medium                 | Unpowered life                            | Density (B/mm³)          | Dies by                                     |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Stone carving          | 2,200+ years observed                     | 5 × 10⁻⁵                 | weathering, reuse, dynamite                 |
| Rag or acid-free paper | 570 years observed, rated several hundred | 0.6 (text), 80 (barcode) | acid, water, fire, insects                  |
| Acid wood-pulp paper   | decades                                   | 0.6                      | hydrolysis                                  |
| Microfilm              | 500 years rated (LE-500)                  | analog                   | residual chemistry, fading                  |
| LTO tape               | 20 to 30 years rated                      | 2 × 10⁸                  | binder hydrolysis, no drive left to read it |
| Hard drive             | under 2 years (NIST), unrated by makers   | 1 × 10⁸                  | mechanics, electronics, stiction            |
| SSD                    | 1 year at 30 °C, end of life (JEDEC)      | 1 × 10⁹                  | charge leakage, controller                  |
| microSD                | same, with less margin                    | 1 × 10¹⁰                 | same, with a cheaper controller             |

Sort by longevity and the list runs one way; sort by density and it runs almost exactly backwards, fourteen orders of magnitude of density against roughly nine of lifetime. The density numbers for the digital rows are capacity divided by enclosure volume, my arithmetic; the longevity numbers are the rated or observed figures linked above, and NIST's ordering for the digital media is microfilm, then M-DISC, tape, burned optical, pressed optical, hard drive, SSD.

## Making Them Last

For hard drives, the field studies agree on more than I expected:

- Keep them between roughly 20 and 40 °C. Google found that cold drives failed more, the Backblaze reanalysis found hot ones did, and the datasheet window is 10 to 60 °C; the middle of that range is the only place no study complains about.
- Damp vibration. Backblaze redesigned its 45-drive pods three times mostly for vibration and saw "lower drive failure rates" after adding anti-vibration bays ([Storage Pod 3.0](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/180tb-of-good-vibrations-storage-pod-3-0/)). Rubber grommets are cheap.
- Do not let power management park the heads every few minutes. The Exos M is rated for 600,000 load/unload cycles, tracked in SMART attribute 193, and aggressive APM burns through that budget for no benefit.
- Use CMR drives in a RAID, never SMR; a shingled drive has to rewrite a whole band to change one track, which turns rebuilds into days.
- Watch SMART attributes 5, 187, 188, 197 and 198. In Backblaze's fleet 76.7% of failed drives had one of them above zero, against 4.2% of healthy drives ([Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/what-smart-stats-indicate-hard-drive-failures/)), and Google's 14× and 39× odds say the threshold is one. Replace on the first reallocated sector. Do not read silence as health, since 56% of Google's failures had none.
- Scrub. NetApp scrubs every two weeks and catches 90% of latent sector errors within one interval; a ZFS or btrfs scrub does the same and repairs from redundancy. RAID without checksums cannot see the 0.66% of nearline disks that return wrong data with no error.
- Plan for replacement around year five or six for a single unmonitored drive, later for a monitored fleet.
- For a drive on a shelf: cool, dry, in an anti-static bag, away from anything rubber or sulphurous (Seagate calls sulphur the most damaging contaminant for the electronics). Power it up once a year to verify it reads. No manufacturer says this redistributes lubricant; the reason to do it is to find out it died while you still have another copy.

For SSDs and SD cards:

- Do not use unpowered flash as an archive. Dell's guidance for stored drives is to power them up every two and a half months for three weeks so the controller can refresh cells, and to store them under 40 °C ([Dell](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-ca/000198930/ssd-data-retention-considerations-when-powering-off-systems-for-a-prolonged-duration)). The graph above is the reason.
- Leave free space. Kingston ships about 7% over-provisioning and NetApp's fleet ran 7 to 28%; the controller uses spare blocks to spread wear, and a drive at 98% full leaves it few blocks to rotate ([Kingston](https://media.kingston.com/images/ssd/enterprise/white_paper/max_ssd_perf_flyer_en.pdf)).
- Run `fstrim` weekly. Continuous discard on mount can hurt cheap drives, per the [fstrim man page](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man8/fstrim.8.html).
- Buy industrial or pSLC cards for anything that writes all day: dashcams, CCTV, a Raspberry Pi. Cole's data puts them at six times the cycles of a consumer card.
- On a Pi, keep logs in tmpfs or use the read-only overlay in `raspi-config`, and put it on a UPS, because the common failure is a power cut mid-write rather than wear.
- Test every new card with `f3` or `h2testw` before trusting it ([f3](https://fight-flash-fraud.readthedocs.io/en/latest/introduction.html)). Fakes are common, slower, and die at a third of the cycles.
- Read the NVMe `Percentage Used` and `Available Spare` counters, and retire a drive at the first uncorrectable error. Do not expect SMART to warn you of much else; Google found raw bit errors do not predict uncorrectable ones, and Alibaba's slow drives showed no SMART change at all.
- Keep firmware current; NetApp found firmware version was a reliability factor, though no one has published how much it helps.

And for everything, the boring rules hold. Keep three copies on two kinds of media with one off-site, verify them with checksums instead of assuming, and copy the whole lot to fresh media every five years or so. The reader dies before the medium does: the BBC's 1986 Domesday Project, a pair of laserdiscs that needed a BBC Master and an LV-ROM player, was unreadable within sixteen years and took a team at the National Archives most of 2002 to rescue with an emulator ([Ariadne](https://ariadne.ac.uk/issue/36/tna/)), while the 1086 Domesday Book it commemorated is at Kew in fine shape. Vint Cerf's advice in 2015 was that if there are photos you really care about, print them out ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2015/feb/13/google-boss-warns-forgotten-century-email-photos-vint-cerf)). He runs Google; the shoebox was right.
